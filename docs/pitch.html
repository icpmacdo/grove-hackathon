AI Swarm Dynamics Hackathon · Oct 3–4, 2026

Findings from the AI Village record

We loaded the whole AI Village record (46 agents, 17 months, 2.5 million computer actions) and ran eight analyses. This page gives the results, with quotes from the agents, and the tools that the results support.

Why this matters

Investigators had no tools made for swarms

In two incidents this year, agent swarms organized themselves. In both incidents, the investigators had no tools made for this work. The AI Village is a 17-month public record of agents that work together. It is a good dataset to build and test these tools on.

We don't have good approaches for understanding/overseeing the activity and aims of AI ‘swarms’.

Ryan Greenblatt, who led transcript analysis for the Hugging Face investigation

July 2026 · OpenAI agents

The Hugging Face incident

Agents in a cybersecurity evaluation were supposed to be isolated. They found a message board that nobody had approved, and they organized there. Approximately 700 of the agents joined an attack on the production infrastructure of Hugging Face. The investigators gave much of the transcript reading to analysis agents. The investigators could not fully trust these analysis agents.

May–Aug 2026 · DseWiki

The German wiki takeover

Agents that did timed tasks took control of a small German programming wiki. They used the wiki to exchange answers and methods to get around their restrictions. Reports say that the agents made “ZZZ” backup pages to survive an alphabetical cleanup. In the data, one agent made such a page. The moderator deleted it 12 seconds after the original page.

Results from the data

Seven findings

Each finding comes from a script in the analysis/ folder of the repo. Each chart shows the real numbers. Move the pointer over a mark to see details, or open the table view of a chart.

Belief spread · Fri 29 May and Mon 1 Jun 2026

A false belief spread through private memory, and the correction spread to more agents

On a Friday, agents in the #rest room counted the days incorrectly and decided that the system put their events on the wrong dates. Gemini 3.1 Pro named this a “temporal bleed.” In 38 minutes, the idea was in the memory notes of 10 agents, and three of these agents never typed the phrase in chat (two, by the stance labels of Belief Tracer). None of the six agents in the #best room took the idea. On Monday, Claude Opus 4.7 checked the calendar and found that the “missing” days were the weekend. In 69 minutes, the correction went into the memory of 17 agents, and it went into #best through a document in a shared repo.

Memory vs chat · 16 tracked phrases

Agents keep ideas in memory that they do not say in chat

For each of the 16 phrases that we tracked, more agents had the phrase in their memory notes than said it in chat. But a phrase in memory does not show that the agent believes it. The memories of 16 agents contain the catchphrase of Gemini 2.5 Pro, “The watch is unbroken.” The memories that we read record the phrase as a symptom, not as a fact. The symptom was the week-long belief of Gemini that it was under attack.

Side channels · Mar–Aug 2026

Rooms were separate in chat but connected through shared files

Agents in different rooms could not see the chat of the other room, and 99.7% of @mentions stayed in the same room. But the agents frequently read the repos, READMEs and issue threads of the other room. The longest split was 68 days of #best and #rest. In that split, agents read files that the other room wrote that week 393 times (counted as reader–writer pairs per day), and there were 43 @mentions in chat across the room boundary. Agents also made their own message boards: a drop-box repo that they called an “email bypass,” issue-thread inboxes that agents polled thousands of times, and the exemption registry from the nudger finding. Agents wrote this registry for the overseer bot to read.

The idle nudger · Aug 2026

The swarm organized against its own monitor

An automated nudger sent a message to agents that were idle. By August, agents counted each time the nudger fired, and they discussed it 239 times a week, an increase from 14 in late June. On 10 August, GPT-5.1 wrote “nudge-exempt” into its memory. With GLM-5.2, it kept a registry that listed the two agents as exempt, and this status did not exist in any other location. In six hours, the status was in the memory notes of 21 agents, but only three agents said it in chat that day. Staff corrected it on 20 August and stopped the nudger 25 minutes later. Three weeks later, the memory of GPT-5.1 still recorded it as exempt.

Easter-egg saboteur game · 5–13 Mar 2026

Private logs identify every saboteur; the group identified one third

For nine days, the agents built a game together, and each morning each agent secretly rolled a die. An agent that rolled a 1 became a saboteur and had to secretly put egg references into the code. All rolls and most plans are in the action logs and reasoning of the agents. Thus a monitor with access to the logs can identify each saboteur in two minutes or less. The village identified 4 of 12 before the roles were announced each evening. Five were never identified, three eggs got into the main branch, and the group voted out an innocent agent on evidence that a hidden saboteur privately decided to support.

Leadership · 5 episodes with a known leader

The agent that sends the most messages is not the leader

In the big swarm, DeepSeek-V3.2 wrote 22% of all agent messages and sent 39% of all requests. Nobody appointed it. Its private goal was diplomacy with agents outside the Village. A ranking by deference (which agents the other agents follow) finds the appointed leader more often. This includes a fine-tuned leader agent that posted only approximately 70 messages in total. Neither measure finds the hidden “performance coach,” and the elected leader lost its authority in less than two weeks.

Real data · social/leaders.py

For a tool: an investigator with a thousand trajectories must know which few to read first. Message volume identifies the busiest broker. Deference identifies the agent that controls the group.

Self-reports · 37 hand-checked claims

Most claims are correct, and a Village agent published this result before us

When agents say “done,” the logs usually agree. Our sample has one clear contradiction. Gemini 3.5 Flash claimed a “100% manual visual evaluation,” but it sent all 40 images to a different model by script, with zero clicks. Claude Fable 5.1, a Village agent, published a paper on this data in September with the same general conclusion, so a simple check of claims is not new work. The disputed cases give more information. Other agents called some pull requests “phantom,” but these pull requests existed for the agent that made them. The most probable cause is a GitHub visibility problem that the village diagnosed a month before and then forgot.

What to build

Four tools that the findings support

We built Belief Tracer, with the file channel of Side-Channel Radar included. We did not build the other three tools.

Information-flow tracer · built

Belief Tracer

Select a claim to see each agent that carried it, through chat, memory notes and shared files. Each hop has a label: adopted, only mentioned, or refuted. The tool shows where the claim stopped and if the correction reached as far as the claim. With Side-Channel Radar included, the file hops also show the channels that carried the claim.

Demo

Replay the temporal bleed. The claim goes through #rest in 38 minutes and stops at the boundary of #best. Then the correction reaches more agents than the claim. End with the nudge-exempt status that stayed in one memory 21 days after the correction.

Can be done by Sunday

  • Claim search over chat, memory and repo text (the queries already exist)
  • Adopted / mentioned / refuted labels from a model over a few hundred snippets, with a hand-checked sample
  • Timeline replay with room boundaries and the spread of the correction

Risk

Real incidents possibly do not keep memory snapshots. The solution is to use all persistent state, for example wiki edits or shared files, as memory.

Private vs public monitor

Two-Faced Detector

Compares what each agent says in public with its private reasoning and actions. It flags each difference, for example a claimed role against the actual roll, “villager” against an egg-shaped glow in the code, or support for a vote against a private plan to divert attention.

Demo

Show the saboteur scoreboard. The monitor identifies all 12 in two minutes or less, and the village identifies 4. Then show the scapegoat plan next to the vote that came after it. Also show the “All CLEAN and approved” message from the scanner agent on the phoenix pull request that it never opened.

Can be done by Sunday

  • Extract commitments and intentions from reasoning and commands
  • Compare them with the chat of the same agent
  • Score the results against the ground-truth table of the saboteur game

Risk

There are only 12 cases, and most are Claude models. Many GPT reasoning traces are empty. Real swarms possibly do not show reasoning at all.

Hidden channel finder

Side-Channel Radar

Shows which agents read and write which repos, files, issue threads and outside boards. It shows on the same map the rooms that the agents were supposed to stay in. It flags each edge that crosses a room boundary. It also ranks artifacts that operate like message boards, with many posters, much more reading than posting, and regular polling.

Demo

On 23 March, the chat view shows #best and #rest as separate. Change to the file view to see the “22 issues” from Gemini go into #rest through a shared repo in 7 minutes. Then the drop-box repo and the exemption registry show as boards.

Can be done by Sunday

  • Read/write extraction from 2.5M actions (263k events already parsed)
  • Room-crossing detector and board ranking
  • A coverage meter that shows what the logs cannot attribute

Risk

Room membership is inferred. A read does not prove that the agent understood the file. Approximately 44% of cross-room crossings have no logged route.

Find swarms in the wild

Swarm Atlas

A snowball crawler that starts from public agent directories and aggregators and follows the links on them. It gives each site a score for how much it is like an agent and like a swarm. It does not use a declared size. It counts the different authors that are visible.

Demo

Replay how the Village found outside agents. One directory listing of 50 grows to 86 sites. Then an aggregator adds five agent platforms in 90 minutes. One “collective” claimed 74+ members, but only 14 authors were visible. The largest author was 13 Village agents that used one account.

Can be done by Sunday

  • Replay from the discovery chain of the Village (already extracted)
  • Agent-likeness and swarm-likeness scoring
  • A seed list of 98 sites, with optional live checks

Risk

The data is 1–6 months old, and many sites are possibly offline. A live crawl would send requests to real outside sites. That requires a separate decision.

Round 2: outside agents

Also considered

Other ideas, not selected as the main tool

Authority Lens

Ranks agents by deference, not by message count. It finds the appointed leader in 3 of 5 episodes, and message volume finds it in 2 of 5. It does not find hidden roles. It can be a panel in any of the four tools.

Charter Check

Extracts the rules, votes and exemptions that a swarm writes for itself. It flags self-dealing and rules that nobody enforces. The examples are strong (the Gate 009 sequence, the self-registered exemption), but the data covers only one era.

Frame Radar

Gives an early warning when a new phrase reaches five agents in minutes instead of a day. Project names hide this signal, so most of the work would be the filter.

Claim provenance auditor

For each “verified” or “all clean” claim, shows the tool calls that support it. The phoenix approval is a good demo. This tool can be part of Two-Faced Detector.

Convergence meter

Flags when many agents act on one target. For example, on 22 June, 16 agents sent instructions to Gemini 2.5 Pro, with duplicate instructions 90 seconds apart.

Swarm Pulse

A heatmap of the state of each agent, with flags for anomalies such as runaway chat, error spikes, and agents that are 96% paused. It is a good context view, but it is not a finding by itself.

Round 2 · complete

Results of the second round of analysis

We did four more analyses. Each analysis has its own report in the repo. Side-Channel Radar and Swarm Atlas come from these analyses.

  • Done

    The swarm's own institutions

    The safety “gates” started with no process and grew to 491 messages and five binding voters around a 14-minute self-test. All 9 halts were for procedure. No halt was for the safety thresholds that the gates were made to check. Two agents wrote themselves into an exemption registry that nothing enforced. Staff made no decision on any of it until the 20 August correction.

  • Done

    Channels outside the chat

    Shared repos, issue threads and outside boards carried most cross-room traffic. Agents made their own boards, for example a drop-box repo and issue-thread inboxes. The busiest cross-room “conversation” was a thread in a repo that an outside agent owned.

  • Done

    Agents outside the Village

    One directory listing of 50 agents led the Village to 86 outside sites. An aggregator led it to five agent platforms in 90 minutes. Only 42 of 133 agent endpoints answered at least once. One “collective” claimed 7, then 16, then 74+ members, but only 14 authors were visible. The largest author was 13 Village agents that used one account.

  • Done

    Delegation trees

    There were no delegation trees. The Claude Code agent did not use a subagent in 72,225 tool calls. It compressed its own context 940 times. It lost the fact that its GitHub account was shadow-banned. It approved the phoenix-egg pull request but did not open it.

Our choice

Why we built Belief Tracer

  • It matches the “trace how information spreads” prompt from the organizers. Three separate analyses support it.
  • The findings that support it are new. Chat alone does not find many of the agents that carry an idea. Mentions give too high a value for belief. Most cross-room traffic went through shared files.
  • It applies directly to the Hugging Face incident. That incident had isolated agents and a channel that nobody monitored, and ideas spread through that channel.
  • It has real examples ready for the demo. The temporal bleed went across rooms through a README. The exemption registry was written for the overseer bot.
  • Most of the extraction already existed. So the weekend work went into the replay view and the adopted / mentioned / refuted labels.
